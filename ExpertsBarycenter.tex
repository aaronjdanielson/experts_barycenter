       via Wasserstein Barycenters}

\begin{abstract}
We study the aggregation of expert probability forecasts over a finite outcome space.
The standard approach, the arithmetic mean (linear opinion pool), is sensitive to extreme opinions: a minority assigning substantial mass to tail outcomes can distort the aggregate away from the majority view.
We propose the Wasserstein barycenter as a robust alternative.
Under the indicator ground cost the barycenter reduces to an $\ell^1$ aggregation problem; for ordered outcome spaces it admits a closed form: the distribution whose CDF is the component-wise median of the individual CDFs, computable without a solver.
We validate the framework across three datasets.
On 94 binary and 113 multi-category questions from the Good Judgment Project (GJP, Years 1--4), the $\ell^1$ barycenter reduces mean Brier score by 12.5\% on binary questions ($p < 0.001$) and 8.7\% on multi-category questions with $K\in\{3,4,5\}$ outcomes ($p < 0.001$); the multi-category reduction grows with $K$, reaching 17.2\% for $K=5$.
On 76 quarters of U.S.\ Survey of Professional Forecasters core CPI histograms (2007--2025), benchmarked against the arithmetic mean, logarithmic pool, and trimmed mean, the barycenter and trimmed mean both achieve lower mean RPS than the arithmetic mean (Wilcoxon $p = 0.005$; DM $p = 0.23$, the latter reflecting variance inflation from the 2021--2022 inflation surge), with the advantage concentrated in low-dispersion quarters (DM $p < 0.001$); the logarithmic pool has the highest P(correct bin) but worst RPS, a divergence explained by the $\ell^1$--$\ell^2$ mismatch between the pooling objective and the scoring rule.
A replication on the ECB Survey of Professional Forecasters confirms the dispersion-conditional pattern on an independent Euro-area panel.
Simulation evidence characterizes the robustness--efficiency tradeoff and identifies conditions under which each method is preferred.
For applied use, we recommend a dispersion-adaptive convex blend of the barycenter and the arithmetic mean: estimated on an expanding historical window, it achieves the lowest out-of-sample RPS of all methods evaluated, improving over the arithmetic mean by 4.5\%, and automatically adapts to the panel's cross-sectional agreement without manual tuning.
\end{abstract}
